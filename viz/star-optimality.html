Why Seven Swaps Is the Law

How we know the shuffler's permute is optimal when every swap must go through the stack top.

the claim vocabulary warm-up: Cayley the star proof guided run try to beat it the literature fine print

1 · The claim

The shuffler's Emission::permute takes a stack whose every slot has a known destination and emits SWAPs until every slot is home. Throughout this page we use one running example: six slots holding a…f, destinations [2 3 4 1 0 5] (the slot at position i must end at position dest[i]; position 5 is the stack top). The algorithm sorts this in seven swaps. The claim under examination: no sequence of EVM swaps whatsoever — however clever — can do it in fewer.

First, what does “optimal” even range over? Picture a giant graph: one node for every possible ordering of the six values (there are 6! = 720), and an edge between two nodes whenever a single legal swap turns one ordering into the other. Sorting is walking from the scrambled node to the sorted node; a strategy is a path; the optimum is the length of the shortest path. Mathematicians call this object a Cayley graph, but nothing beyond “graph of states, edges are moves” is needed here.

Which edges exist depends on the machine. A CPU with random access may swap any two positions. The EVM may not: SWAPn exchanges the top with the slot n below it — every edge touches the top. Draw one vertex per stack position and connect two positions when they may be swapped, and the CPU gives you the complete graph, while the EVM gives you a star: the top at the center, everything else a spoke. This picture is why the literature calls EVM-style swaps star transpositions.

which pairs may swap · CPU vs EVM

Proving a strategy optimal always has two halves. The upper bound is easy to believe: run the algorithm, count its swaps — seven. The lower bound is the interesting half: an argument that every path through the state graph, all 720-node-many of them, has length at least seven. You cannot check paths one by one; you need an invariant. That invariant is the whole story of this page.

2 · Vocabulary, slowly

2.1 A permutation is a destination map

Forget the values on the stack for a moment; only the routing plan matters. The plan is an array dest where dest[i] says where the slot currently at position i must end up. Two properties make such an array a permutation: every position gets a destination (totality) and no two positions share one (injectivity). Equivalently: dest contains each of 0…n−1 exactly once. When dest[i] = i for all i, the plan is the identity — nothing to do, stack sorted.

A physical swap of positions x and y does something very simple to the plan: it swaps the entries dest[x] and dest[y]. The slots traded places, so they traded destinations-of-current-position. Sorting is the game of turning dest into the identity using entry-swaps — the values themselves are just along for the ride.

2.2 Cycles: the arrows always come home

Start at any position and follow the plan: i → dest[i] → dest[dest[i]] → … Because each position has exactly one outgoing arrow and exactly one incoming arrow, this walk can never branch and never dead-end; in a finite set it must eventually revisit its start (it cannot re-enter the middle of its own trail — that would give some position two incoming arrows). The loop you traced is a cycle. Positions not on it form their own cycles, so the whole plan splits into disjoint cycles — uniquely. A position with dest[i] = i is a one-element cycle, a fixed point.

Our running example dest = [2 3 4 1 0 5] splits as (0 2 4) · (1 3) · (5): a 3-cycle, a 2-cycle, and the top sitting home. We write c for the number of cycles counting fixed points — here c = 3. Sorted means every cycle is a fixed point: c = n.

2.3 The surgery lemma: a swap merges or splits, nothing else

Here is the single most load-bearing fact, and it is checkable by tracing arrows. Swap the plan entries at positions x and y and watch the cycle structure:

  • If x and y sit in the same cycle, the cycle splits into two. Intuition: the cycle is a ring of arrows through both x and y; rerouting the two arrows that left x and y crosses the ring into two smaller rings.
  • If x and y sit in different cycles, the two cycles merge into one. Same picture in reverse: the rerouted arrows splice two rings into one.
the surgery lemma · one swap = merge or split, always

Note what this rules out: no swap can create two new cycles at once, dissolve two at once, or leave the count unchanged. c moves by exactly ±1 per swap, no exceptions. You can verify this live in the sandbox below — the readout names every move a merge or a split.

3 · Warm-up: optimality when any pair may swap

Before the star, solve the CPU. Define the potential Φ₀(dest) = n − c. Three observations, and note the shape of the argument as much as the content — the star proof will reuse the shape exactly:

  • Sorted ⇔ Φ₀ = 0. Sorted means all n positions are fixed points, i.e. c = n.
  • Every swap changes Φ₀ by exactly ±1 — that is the surgery lemma verbatim.
  • Therefore every sorting sequence has length ≥ Φ₀(start). A quantity that must travel from Φ₀ to 0 in steps of ±1 needs at least Φ₀ steps. (Programmer's framing: Φ₀ is a loop variant that no instruction can decrease by more than 1.)

That is the lower bound. The upper bound is a strategy that finds a −1 move every time: pick any position x not yet home and swap it with dest[x] — a same-cycle swap that splits off a fixed point. Since a split is available whenever anything is unsorted, the strategy uses exactly Φ₀ = n − c swaps, meeting the bound. Equality; optimal.

This result — minimum swaps = n − c — is due to Cayley (1849), “Note on the theory of permutations”, and n − c is nowadays called the Cayley distance. Our example: n − c = 6 − 3 = 3. A CPU sorts it in three exchanges. The EVM demonstrably cannot — and quantifying that gap is next.

4 · The star proof

4.1 Why n − c stops being reachable

Φ₀ is still a valid lower bound on the EVM — star swaps are swaps. But it is no longer achievable. Take the tiny plan (1 3) with the top at 5: two slots need to trade, the top is a bystander. n − c = 1, yet no single SWAPn sorts it — every swap moves the top, and the top is already home. You must spend a swap injecting the top into the cycle (a merge: Φ₀ goes up), then two placements. Three swaps, not one. The obstruction is structural: a cycle that does not contain the top can only be reached by first merging the top into it, and merges are exactly the moves Φ₀ counts as backwards.

4.2 Repairing the potential

So the true cost must charge those entries up front. Let b = the number of nontrivial cycles (length ≥ 2) that do not contain the top — the cycles that will each demand one paid entry. Define:

Φ(dest)  =  (n − c)  +  2b

Check the anchor first: sorted ⇔ c = n and b = 0 ⇔ Φ = 0. And on the running example: n − c = 3, and both nontrivial cycles avoid the top so b = 2, giving Φ = 3 + 4 = 7. The number we are trying to explain has appeared out of the definition.

Why the factor 2? A merge into a foreign cycle improves b by one (that cycle now contains the top) but worsens n − c by one — for the potential to credit entries as progress, the b term must outweigh the c term: hence weight 2, net −1. What remains is to check that no star swap can beat −1, which is a finite case analysis over what a swap (top, x) can structurally do.

4.3 The case table — every star swap moves Φ by exactly ±1

Every star swap is a surgery involving the top's cycle and x's cycle. There are exactly seven shapes it can take. Write t for the top; “trivial” means a fixed point.

Walk any row: the Δ(n−c) column is the surgery lemma (±1 by merge/split); the Δ2b column just asks whether a topless nontrivial cycle was created or destroyed. Every row nets exactly ±1. There is no row with −2, and no row with 0. (The three good rows are worth internalising: placing a slot, entering a fresh cycle, and fusing two cycles through the top are equally productive — which is why optimal solutions are far from unique.)

4.4 Assembling the theorem

Lower bound. Φ starts at Φ(dest), must reach 0, and moves by ±1 per swap. Every sorting sequence — greedy, clairvoyant, or perverse — has length at least Φ(dest). No enumeration of the 720-node graph required; the invariant does all the work.

Upper bound. The shuffler's greedy always has a −1 row available: if the top is not home, its cycle is nontrivial and contains t, so place applies (swap the top with its slot's destination); if the top is home but work remains, some topless nontrivial cycle exists, so enter applies. Greedy therefore finishes in exactly Φ(dest) swaps.

Lower = upper. Φ(dest) = (n − c) + 2b is the exact optimum, and permute attains it. Regrouped per cycle this is the formula quoted everywhere: a cycle of length ℓ containing the top costs ℓ − 1; every other nontrivial cycle costs ℓ + 1. For the running example, (3+1) + (2+1) = 7.

A bonus corollary from “±1 per step”: any sorting sequence has the same parity as Φ. You can take 7, 9, or 11 swaps on our example — never 8. (This is the permutation's sign in disguise: each swap flips it.)

6 · Try to beat seven

Now the lower bound as an experience. Click any non-top cell to swap it with the top. Any strategy you like. The readout classifies each of your moves against the case table and tracks Φ. You will find: good moves exist in abundance (there are usually several), but nothing ever moves Φ by more than 1 — so the sorted banner cannot appear before seven swaps have been spent. Try to churn, bury, strand; try to be clever. The invariant does not care.

Two remarks tie the strands together. First, Vaughan & Portier's minimality characterisation says extra cost above their path-length bound arises exactly when a fixed point sits on an active route — on the star, every route runs through the center, so a homed top blocking remaining cycles is that obstruction, and our enter row is their C-step in EVM clothing. Second, the star and the path are the only swap-network trees where greedy is proven optimal; the EVM landed on one of the two lucky topologies.

8 · Fine print for the shuffler

The theorem above is exactly true for the model it states: distinct values, unlimited reach, pure permutation. Emission::permute lives in a messier world, and each deviation is worth naming honestly:

  • Equal values beat the formula — legitimately. If two slots hold equal values, destinations between them are interchangeable, and the “permutation” is really a choice among several. permute's preprocessing and its swapDestinations retags pick, on the fly, the assignment with the cheapest Φ — dissolving cycles that would only shuttle identical values. The optimum is then min over assignments of Φ, and retagging costs zero swaps.
  • Reach is finite. SWAPn stops at depth 16, so the EVM is a star with shortened spokes. A needed slot beyond reach has no legal edge at all; permute reports Blocked and the planner drops regenerable slots and retries. The optimality theorem simply does not speak about this regime — no closed-form theory does, which is why the shuffler handles it with iteration rather than a formula.
  • Φ prices the whole pipeline. permuteAtLowestHeight runs when the stack is shortest (spokes at their longest, in reach terms), and parks not-yet-placeable slots in holes so that later generation swaps double as free placements — moves chosen so the eventual permutation has small Φ and its swaps do double duty. The potential is a good mental meter for reading all of the emission code, not just permute.
